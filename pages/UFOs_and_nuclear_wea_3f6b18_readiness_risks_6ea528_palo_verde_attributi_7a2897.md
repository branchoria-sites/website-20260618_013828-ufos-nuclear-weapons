## Introduction

The 2019 drone incursions at the Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station in Arizona are one of the clearest examples of a problem that often gets lost in discussions about [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) objects near sensitive facilities: [detection]({{ 'detection/' | relative_url }}) is not the same thing as attribution. Security personnel knew that drones were present. They observed their movements, reported the incidents, and involved multiple government agencies. Yet the operators were never publicly identified, their intentions remained unclear, and the event exposed limits in the ability of authorities to respond to unknown aerial activity around critical infrastructure. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.twz.com/34800/the-night-a-drone-swarm-descended-on-palo-verde-nuclear-power-plant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: twz.com">[The War Zone]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">twz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the night a drone swarm descended on palo verde nuclear power plant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War ZoneThe Night A Mysterious Drone Swarm Descended On Palo...30 Jul 2020 — Rodriguez reported that a number of drones were flying...</span></span></span>

Within the broader subject of [UFOs and nuclear weapons]({{ 'ufos-and-nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}), Palo Verde is significant precisely because it does not require extraordinary explanations. The case demonstrates how conventional [drones]({{ 'drones/' | relative_url }}) can create uncertainty around a nuclear facility and how difficult it can be to determine who is responsible, what they are doing, and whether they pose a genuine threat. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csgmidwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NRC-Drones-Memo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csgmidwest.org">[CSG Midwest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csgmidwest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CSG MidwestDrones and Nuclear Power Plant SecurityOctober 27, 2020 — The NRC asks nuclear power plant licensees to voluntarily report any...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2020</span></span></span>

## What Was Reported Near the Plant

The incident occurred on the nights of 29 and 30 September 2019 at the Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station, the largest nuclear power plant in the United States. According to Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) records released through Freedom of Information Act requests, security personnel observed multiple drones operating near Unit 3 and other protected areas of the site. Reports described groups of approximately five or six drones during at least one of the incursions. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.twz.com/34800/the-night-a-drone-swarm-descended-on-palo-verde-nuclear-power-plant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: twz.com">[The War Zone+2Forbes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">twz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the night a drone swarm descended on palo verde nuclear power plant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War ZoneThe Night A Mysterious Drone Swarm Descended On Palo...30 Jul 2020 — Rodriguez reported that a number of drones were flying...</span></span></span>

The activity was not a brief overflight. Internal accounts indicated that some of the drones remained in the area for extended periods, moving around the facility in ways that attracted security attention. Officials regarded the behaviour as unusual enough to notify federal authorities and initiate broader [reporting]({{ 'reporting/' | relative_url }}) procedures. The FBI, Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), Department of Homeland Security, local law enforcement and the NRC all became involved in subsequent investigations. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.twz.com/34800/the-night-a-drone-swarm-descended-on-palo-verde-nuclear-power-plant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: twz.com">[The War Zone+2The Debrief]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">twz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the night a drone swarm descended on palo verde nuclear power plant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War ZoneThe Night A Mysterious Drone Swarm Descended On Palo...30 Jul 2020 — Rodriguez reported that a number of drones were flying...</span></span></span>

What makes Palo Verde notable is not merely that drones appeared near a nuclear plant. Drone sightings around critical infrastructure have occurred elsewhere. Rather, the Palo Verde case produced unusually detailed documentation showing that trained observers saw the aircraft, tracked them, and still struggled to determine who controlled them or why they were there. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/davidhambling/2020/07/30/drone-swarm-invaded-palo-verde-nuclear-power-plant/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forbes.com">[Forbes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forbes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">drone swarm invaded palo verde nuclear power plant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forbes&#x27;Drone Swarm&#x27; Invaded Palo Verde Nuclear Power Plant...30 Jul 2020 — A number of small drones flew around a restricted area at Pal...</span></span></span>

## Why Operator Attribution Mattered

The central question was never simply, “Were there drones?” The available evidence strongly suggests there were. The harder question was who was flying them.

Several possibilities existed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Recreational operators acting irresponsibly.
* Commercial operators conducting unauthorised activity.
* Hobbyists unaware of restrictions.
* Individuals intentionally testing security responses.
* Actors gathering intelligence about the facility.
* A coordinated exercise or operation that had not been properly communicated to site personnel.

</div>

The challenge was that observation alone could not distinguish among these explanations. A drone carrying a camera can look identical whether its purpose is photography, curiosity, criminal reconnaissance or intelligence collection. The same flight pattern can support multiple interpretations. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csgmidwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NRC-Drones-Memo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csgmidwest.org">[CSG Midwest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csgmidwest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CSG MidwestDrones and Nuclear Power Plant SecurityOctober 27, 2020 — The NRC asks nuclear power plant licensees to voluntarily report any...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2020</span></span></span>

This distinction is critical for nuclear security. A security team can react differently if it knows a drone belongs to a nearby contractor than if it suspects a hostile reconnaissance effort. Without attribution, decision-makers are forced to operate under uncertainty. The event becomes not just an [airspace]({{ 'airspace/' | relative_url }}) issue but an intelligence problem. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csgmidwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NRC-Drones-Memo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csgmidwest.org">[CSG Midwest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csgmidwest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CSG MidwestDrones and Nuclear Power Plant SecurityOctober 27, 2020 — The NRC asks nuclear power plant licensees to voluntarily report any...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2020</span></span></span>

Internal discussions reported after the incident reflected concern that the drones appeared capable of conducting a deliberate survey of the site. However, concern is not proof. The operators were not publicly identified, and no publicly available evidence established a hostile motive. The inability to move from observation to attribution was therefore the most important [unresolved]({{ 'unresolved/' | relative_url }}) aspect of the case. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/davidhambling/2020/09/07/dozens-more-drone-incursions-over-us-nuclear-power-plants-revealed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forbes.com">[Forbes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forbes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dozens More Mystery Drone Incursions Over U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">7, 2020 — In the September incidents, a swarm of five or six large drones flew over the Unit 3 nuclear reactor at Palo Ver...</span></span></span>

## Why Seeing a Drone Did Not Mean Controlling the Situation

One of the most revealing lessons from Palo Verde was that detecting an aircraft does not automatically provide the means to stop it.

At the time, nuclear plant security personnel had significant responsibilities for protecting facilities but limited authority to interdict aircraft. NRC guidance noted that plant security forces generally could not simply shoot down drones or employ aggressive countermeasures on their own authority. Reports were instead passed to federal and local agencies responsible for aviation and law enforcement. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csgmidwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NRC-Drones-Memo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csgmidwest.org">[CSG Midwest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csgmidwest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CSG MidwestDrones and Nuclear Power Plant SecurityOctober 27, 2020 — The NRC asks nuclear power plant licensees to voluntarily report any...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2020</span></span></span>

That creates a timing problem. Small drones can appear, conduct surveillance, and leave before investigators locate the operator. Even if authorities know a drone is present, finding the pilot may require tracking radio signals, identifying launch locations, coordinating multiple agencies and overcoming legal constraints. By the time those processes occur, the aircraft may be gone. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csgmidwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/NRC-Drones-Memo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csgmidwest.org">[CSG Midwest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csgmidwest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CSG MidwestDrones and Nuclear Power Plant SecurityOctober 27, 2020 — The NRC asks nuclear power plant licensees to voluntarily report any...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2020</span></span></span>
